# Manifesto

> Integrations don't stall on APIs. They stall on definitions nobody owns.

## What I believe

Two systems that don't talk usually belong to two teams that don't talk. Melvin Conway wrote it in
1968: organizations design systems that mirror their communication structures. Fixing the systems
starts with fixing who owns the conversation.

An integration is a product with users on both sides. Production planning, the operator at the
terminal and the manager reading the numbers are its users, even if none of them ever sees the API.

## The contract is the product

The code on each side is the vendors' job. What crosses between them is a decision, and decisions
need an owner. Writing that down is the most useful product work in an integration.

## No made-up numbers

A default value on a shop floor terminal looks exactly like a real one. An order that waits for its
data is visible. A wrong time is not.

## Ask the question the floor would ask

"Is the catalog complete?" is a question about the ERP. "Can every order the floor will receive be
sent as it is?" is a question about the product. The second one has a much shorter answer.

## The word "everything" needs a scope

When two teams hear the same promise, they each fill it with their own scope. Writing the scope
down costs one message. Not writing it costs trust.

## The rule belongs to the people who know it

Production planning knew which orders mattered to the floor. My first version got it wrong. The
product work was putting their rule in a place where both systems had to follow it.
